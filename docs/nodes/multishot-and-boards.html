Multi-Shot & boards

Where Brut stops being a one-image-at-a-time tool. Multi-Shot explores a scene from many angles at once; the boards are the surfaces where you review, curate and route the results.

Every board shares one review gesture: hover a tile and click its 🔍 to open the lightbox — the image fitted to your screen, wheel-zoom into the pixels, and ←/→ (or the on-screen arrows) paging through the whole board like a classical gallery. Clicking the image itself does nothing (no accidental tabs while panning the canvas); the full-resolution files live in the project's output folder.

#Multi-Shot

A POV/composition explorer. Wire your base image(s) into a Multi-Shot node, pick an exploration scenario and a shot count, and a planner (Gemini by default) writes N distinct shot prompts from your base images. Then N image-to-image jobs fill a contact sheet on a resizable node, several render at once.

  • Scenarios: Editorial, Ballsy, Morning → Evening, Four Seasons, Dutch Masters, Product and Product · Lifestyle (below), the five Lifestyle scenarios (Lifestyle, Lifestyle · Home, Lifestyle · Hospitality, Lifestyle · Work, Lifestyle · Public: below), and a Custom scenario that takes its brief from the node's Notes field. (Older scenarios are retired: the climate ones, Cold North, Mediterranean and Desert, whose weather registers now live as V2 deck cards, and Clichés. A saved graph that used one keeps working and shows it as retired.)
  • Notes live on the node — a text box right under the scenario picker steers the planner on any scenario, and for Custom it is the brief. Wired text (a Prompt or Refine node into the notes input) still works and both flow to the planner — wire when the text has a life of its own, type when it's a one-off. Pair Custom with the Scenario maker Refine preset for a strong brief.
  • 💾 Save as preset appears under non-empty notes — it saves the text as a prompt preset in the global library, loadable from any Prompt node in any project.
  • It works on any subject, not only buildings. The planner first reads what your pictures show (a building, an interior, a landscape, a product, a fashion or portrait shoot) and says so in the Activity panel. Every shot keeps that subject as it is, keeps the background at its true size and distance, and never adds buildings the pictures don't show. A building seen far off in one picture stays far off. On a fashion or portrait shoot the people are the subject of every frame.
  • Nodes made before this rule keep the old one until you choose otherwise, so saved sheets stay as they are and nothing is planned again on its own. The old rule assumed a building and could put one into pictures that had none. Such a node shows a line, Newer planner rules, with a Use them button; its next run plans the shots again (the run tells you what that costs first). Switching a V1 node to V2 takes the new rules too.
  • Multi-Shot explores points of view — the look comes after. For grade/style transfer, don't feed a look reference into the exploration (feeding a look into the exploration pulls every composition toward it and makes sheets repetitive). Instead, run the shots you keep through a Compose node: scene = the shot, reference = the look image, prompt = the built-in Grade match preset. You pay once per keeper instead of per exploration, and 1-to-1 transfer is far stronger.
  • The shot list is kept between runs, so unchanged shots aren't re-planned or re-paid. 🎲 rolls a new shot list.
  • Per-tile controls — ↻ re-rolls one tile (only that tile re-pays; during a run, ↻ on any tile queues it and the node runs once more right after, so several tiles can be re-rolled at once), ✓ / ✗ mark keepers and misses, 🕘 re-pins a past result for that specific tile.
  • Curation flows downstream — ✓ shots flow out the main port (or all non-✗ if none are ticked), taking effect on the next run (free).

The contact sheet uses a tidy, fixed column count from the shot count (≤4 → 2×2, ≤9 → 3-wide, else 4-wide), so it stays neat at any node size. The node's corner grip sets its width only: the height always follows the content — the Notes box (eight lines minimum, drag its own corner for more), the controls and the sheet's rows of squares — so the tiles are never squashed. The eye button beside New shot list folds the sheet away (the shots are kept, the node shrinks to its controls; the Shot Board is the place to review them large).

#V2 — the surprise machinery

New Multi-Shot nodes plan with V2; a small V1/V2 rollout next to the title switches generations (graphs made before V2 keep V1). V2 keeps your sheets from repeating the same ideas job after job — the same prop, the same weather trick:

  • The card deck. Every plan draws a hand of ingredients from the scenario's pools (a light register, a life event, a prop domain, …) for the set to use. The hand is shown as chips on the node before you ever run — visible surprise — and the little 🎲 beside the chips redraws it without starting a run.
  • Pick your hand. Random is the default, but the ✎ beside the chips opens the picker: one row per pool, each set to random or a card of your choice. Pinned cards always ride the hand (shown as accent chips; wild cards are pickable at any dial level), the Surprise dial fills the remaining slots randomly, and 🎲 redraws only those. Pin everything — or pin on Classic, which draws nothing on its own — and the hand is fully yours. Picks belong to their scenario's deck, so switching scenario clears them.
  • The Surprise dial. Classic (no cards: the scenario's brief alone) · Fresh (the default hand) · Crazy (a bigger hand including each pool's wild cards, plus a push toward the never-published).
  • Avoid recent motifs (off by default). Brut remembers the distinctive ideas of your recent plans, per scenario and across all projects. Tick the box and new plans steer away from them — the direct cure for "the same idea on every job". The memory ages out, so retired classics eventually return.

V2 also makes the planner model selectable — the text model that writes the shot list (the node's Provider/Model picks stay the image model that renders them). The default is Gemini 3.8 Flash; different planners have different instincts, which is itself a variety lever, and models with a reasoning dial expose it here too.

Nothing is rendered until the shot list is valid: a planner reply that can't be read stops the run with a clear message before any image is paid for. Planning calls cost cents.

#Product: one object, many settings

Wire the pictures of one object (a chair, a lamp, a watch, a shelf, a bag, a device) and pick Product. The planner reads what the object is and plans a presentation set: a hero frame (the product whole, three-quarter, the light shaping it), a straight-on frame on a plain ground, close details of a material or a joint, the product in use at human scale, and at least two different settings it names concretely (a studio sweep, a living room, a terrace, a workshop bench, a shop display). The product stays exactly what your pictures show, at its true size; the setting changes from frame to frame, which is the point of the set. The planner never invents a side or a detail your pictures do not show: a frame that would need the hidden side keeps it turned away or out of frame. Several pictures of the product (the front, the back, a detail) give it more to work with.

The Surprise dial deals cards here as on the other scenarios. The pools are the setting (a studio sweep, at home, outdoors, in the shop, on the maker's bench), the light (window light, hard sun, one soft source, a low warm lamp), the framing (hero three-quarter, straight on, macro detail, in use), the people (nobody, hands only, one person using it) and the register (catalogue, editorial, documentary). ✎ pins the ones you want.

Product · Lifestyle: the product in a real place, used by real people. Pick it and the node grows a product inlet: wire the object's pictures there (one or several sides of it) and the pictures of the place on place (the base inlet, renamed). The planner reads both, proposes the one or two people who would really use this product in this place (the household of a home, a guest and a waiter at a hotel, a maker at a bench), and the run stops on Meet the cast like every lifestyle scenario. Each shot then shows the product in use or in place at its true size, handled or approached by someone from the cast: a hero frame with the product whole in the place, close frames of it in hands, a wide frame where it is small and the place tells the scale, a detail of its material beside something of the place. The product stays exactly as your pictures show it, the place as your pictures show it, and the people as their sheets show them. The photographer dial and the angle work as on the other lifestyle scenarios. A set with the product alone, in settings the planner names, is the plain Product scenario.

#Lifestyle scenarios: people, cast first

Five scenarios where people are the subject, with the place: the set shows the place as it is lived. They plan on V2.

  • Lifestyle: the general one. The planner decides who appears from what it reads: a few recurring people, the people passing through, or both.
  • Lifestyle · Home: one household across the whole set, chosen to fit the place (a couple and a child in a family house, a retired couple in a single-storey villa, one young professional in a city flat). The developer's sale: a week of their life here.
  • Lifestyle · Hospitality: the same few staff throughout, a different guest party in each frame. Arrival, breakfast, dinner service, turndown, the night shift, wherever the pictures show the place for it.
  • Lifestyle · Work: an office, a studio, a campus, a workshop. A few recurring people (the team this place would really hold), colleagues, visitors and couriers passing through once, from the morning arrival to the end of the day.
  • Lifestyle · Public: a plaza, a park, a school, a museum, a station, a shop. The public at its real density for the hour, every age this place would draw, and the few people who belong here (a guard, a vendor, a teacher) recurring where the place has them.

The stock moments of a brochure (the golden-hour toast, laughter on the sofa, the child running on the lawn) are left out of every lifestyle set: a meal, a coffee, an arrival are welcome when this place gives them a reason.

The Surprise dial picks the photographer. On these scenarios the dial deals no cards; it chooses who takes the pictures, and the cast stays the same whatever you pick:

  • Classic: a precise, classical photographer. Calm, exact frames: the camera level, the verticals straight, natural light at its best hour, the people in their real number and placed with care. Elegant, no tricks.
  • Fresh: an avant-garde photographer. The set follows an angle read from your pictures (the night porter's round, the life of the place seen from under the deck) as a thread, with unexpected vantages inside the views your pictures cover and light used as a subject.
  • Crazy: a restless young photographer. A bolder angle and daring frames (down from a gallery a picture shows, flash at dusk, motion blur, hard crops), still real photographs: true scale, nothing surreal.

On Fresh and Crazy the people stay recognizable in most frames; a back or a silhouette appears at most once in a set.

Every photographer shoots a photo story. Whichever one you pick, the set is planned like a reportage, not an architecture shoot: every frame has a moment (something happening between people, or between a person and the place), a point of view often close to the people, a foreground that gives the frame depth, lenses with a photographic look, light with direction. The distances vary across the set, from a close frame to a wide one where the people are small. The people come in their real number: the planner writes how many are in each frame, as the hour and the program would really bring them (a restaurant at dinner service is mostly full, a station at the morning peak holds dozens), and an almost empty frame says why. A person far from the camera stays small, their face unread.

Place pictures per shot. Every shot is sent with the character sheets of the people in it, then the pictures of the place. The planner ranks the place pictures for each shot (the one the shot stands in first), and the shot carries as many as the image model takes: some models take fewer pictures in a request than others (Ideogram 4.5 takes five in all, Seedream and FLUX 3 ten, Nano Banana 2.1 fourteen, GPT Image 2.5 sixteen), and a frame goes out with the closest ones when they do not all fit. The node's Place pictures per shot select sets a count of your own, all, the closest 3 or the closest 5: in our tests fewer pictures held a person better and let the building drift more, so measure on your set. The shot list in the Activity panel says which pictures each frame went with when the list was cut.

Pictures at 4K max. A Multi-Shot node sends copies of its pictures resized to 4096 px on their long side when they are larger (a 5000 by 4000 render becomes 4096 by 3277); the originals stay on disk, in the ledger and in History, and a picture within the cap goes as it is. This lets every model take the same set (Seedream takes a request up to 64 MB; FLUX 3 takes a picture up to 16 megapixels and gets a 16-megapixel copy of a larger one), and the models we tried read a 4K copy like the original. Untick the box under the dial to send the originals; nodes made before this box keep sending their originals until it is ticked. The box applies to this node only: Adjust, Layers and Conform always work on the picture's own pixels.

The frames stay on your pictures. Your pictures are the only truth the image model has about the place, so every frame is shot from a view they cover: the camera moves a few steps, up or down, changes its lens or its subject, and the people and the moment make the frame new. A side, a room or a view no picture shows is not asked for; at most one frame per set leaves the covered views, and then as a close frame where the place is a fragment around the people. Dusk and night frames are kept to two per set, each from a view a picture covers closely, so the place is relit rather than redrawn. With a single picture, every frame is that view at another distance, height or crop, with another moment in it: more pictures of the place give the set more views. In our tests every frame that held the building was shot from a view a picture covered, and every reinvented building came from a view no picture showed or from a dusk relight. ✎ still pins a card from the scenario's deck when you want one (a lazy Sunday, a winter afternoon).

The angle. Once the cast is approved, the planner reads your pictures and the cast and proposes three angles for Fresh and three for Crazy (one short call, a few cents, made once for both). An angle comes from what is done at this place and by whom (the program, the roles, the routines, the features only this place has), never from a device laid over it (a shadow clock, a day told by the light). Brut draws one, so the set does not always start from the planner's first idea. On the run that proposes them, the run stops on Pick the angle: the three angles with their ideas, the drawn one marked; pick one, or ↻ Propose others for three new ones, then Plan the set on this angle. Stop ends the run there with nothing more spent; the proposals stay on the node, and the next run plans on the one the Angle line shows without asking. The angle is a thread through the set: it shapes about half the frames and leaves the others free, so a set still shows different people, situations and parts of the place. The Angle line on the node shows it with its idea written under it: pick another of the three from the list, 🎲 moves to the next one, ↻ asks for three new ones right away. With the cast approved and no angles yet, ↻ Propose angles reads them before any shot, so you can choose first. Switching the dial between Classic, Fresh and Crazy plans the shots again for the same people; no new casting, no new sheets. The angles are proposed again when the pictures, the notes or the cast change.

How a lifestyle run goes. The planner reads the place first and proposes the cast: each person gets a role here ("the mother", "the night porter"), never a name, with their sex, age, build and what their role makes them wear. Brut then draws each recurring person's face, hair color, haircut and palette from the same lists as the Character Lab card, so two roles never come out as the same person: a household shares one heritage and its children take after a parent. Then the run stops on "Meet the cast" before any shot is planned:

  • One card per person. Edit the role, the age, the build, the clothes; change the heritage, the face, the hair or the palette from their lists, or 🎲 Draw again for a new face, hair and palette (the role and age stay). ✕ drops someone, + Add a person adds one, and a person can be set to appears once: a passing guest stays a few words in their one shot, with no face to draw and no sheet (set them back to recurs to give them both).
  • Make the sheets. Every recurring person needs a character sheet before the shots: one 16:9 picture of them (a bust and four full-body views) made with the Character Lab's own recipe and model (GPT Image 2.5 Flare at 4K, xhigh: about $0.18 a sheet). Click a sheet to see it large, then ←/→ through the whole cast. ↻ New take makes another take of the same person. From a file… or Elements… uses a picture you already have instead (a sheet from the Character Lab, a photo). The sheet's text is written by Gemini; when Gemini declines to describe a person (it does for some children), the scenario's planner writes that sheet instead, and the Activity panel says so. If both decline, the card says what to change: the clothes or the build, a new draw, or a picture of your own.
  • Approve and the shots are planned for this cast (on Fresh and Crazy, after the angle); each shot is sent with the sheets of the people in it only, so the same people come back in every frame. Stop ends the run there: nothing is planned or spent. A run whose cast is unchanged goes on without asking; edit the cast and the next run asks again.
  • Save to Elements. A person with a sheet has a Save to Elements button on their card, and the footer saves everyone at once: each becomes a cast element in your Elements vault (the sheet as its picture, the role as its name, the one-line description as its descriptor), marked ✓ in Elements on the card. Bring them back into another set from the Elements… button on a card, or wire them onto the cast inlet from the Elements panel. Saving changes nothing in the cast or its approval.

The cast stays on the node: wiring other pictures or changing the notes changes the shots, not the people (the node says when the cast was made from other pictures). 🎲 beside the cast on the node casts again on the next run; 👥 Meet opens the cast at any time, outside a run. The people in each frame show as small labels on its tile.

The cast inlet. Wire character sheets or photos into cast (a Character Lab output, an Image In, an Elements picture): those people join the cast as they are, and the planner casts the rest around them.

In the Editorial Sheet workflow. The Editorial Sheet card carries the same scenarios on its Scenario knob: pick Lifestyle, Home, Hospitality, Work, Public or Product · Lifestyle and the closed card runs the whole flow. The run stops on Meet the cast exactly as on the node, and the card gains a line under its knobs: the cast with its sheets, 👥 Meet (the cast at any time, outside a run) and 🎲 (a new cast on the next run), the photographer the Surprise knob picked, and on Fresh and Crazy the Angle line with its list, 🎲 and ↻. Place pictures per shot and Pictures at 4K max are knobs on the card; the first is greyed on the editorial scenarios, where it means nothing. A cast inlet appears on the lifestyle scenarios and a product inlet on Product · Lifestyle, where the base inlet reads place; they take the same wires as the node's. The card ships with pictures at 4K max on, like a new node. Each shot on the card carries ↻ (a new take of that shot, the others kept) and 🕘 (its past takes) on hover; a Shot Board wired from sheet stays the review surface for ✓ and ✗.

The planner. These scenarios plan on Claude Sonnet 5.5 at high reasoning when the node's Planner is left on its default (it read the places best in our tests; a casting call costs about two cents, a shot plan about five). A seat without access to it plans on the usual default and says so in the Activity panel. A pick of your own on the Planner select always wins.

What it costs, measured. A first set of nine shots on Nano Banana Pro at 1K with three or four sheets came to about $1.80–2.00; planning again with the same cast, about $1.26. In our tests the household stayed the same people from frame to frame with sheets, and not with words alone; no sheet's grey background or pose leaked into a shot. A small detail on one side of the head (a single earring) does not carry into the shots, even from a correct sheet.

Every shot carries every picture of the place, with the sheets of the people in it. In our tests the way the sheets are sent decided whether a person held: a waiter came out as himself in 4 takes of 6 after we changed it, against 1 of 6 before. Sending only the one or two place pictures a shot needed held him in 6 of 6, but the building drifted and the people grew too big for the place, so every picture rides. If a person still comes out as someone else, ↻ on that tile tries again with the same sheet.

#Shot Board

The review surface for a Multi-Shot node. Wire the Multi-Shot shots output into a Shot Board and get a large captioned grid — the prompt under each image, ✓ / ✗ / ↻ act on the Multi-Shot node. Each tile has its own output plug so you can route one chosen shot into Compose or Image→Video. The board re-emits the curated pool from its bottom port. Tiles show each shot at its real aspect (a 9:16 batch is a column of portrait frames, never a 4:3 crop); the Multi-Shot node's own compact sheet stays square thumbnails — the board is where you judge the actual frame.

#Reference Board

Makes the crew's stills pool visible. Every image you wire in becomes a numbered tile — and the tile number is the global still number the cinema agents refer to, which kills the invisible wire-order problem.

  • ↑ / ↓ reorder (the plan refers to the stills by number, so reordering changes what a wire carries: what you see is what flows).
  • ✕ hide a tile / ↺ restore it in place.
  • Each tile has its own output plug, addressing one still by its board number.
  • A name field under each tile shows the name a prompt downstream writes as @name (the picture's own name after its file, or the node that made it, until you type one; see Image In). Typing a name on a tile names the picture on the Image In that holds it; a render wired straight into the board is named on the board itself.

Free and local — it's just a lens on the images you already have.
